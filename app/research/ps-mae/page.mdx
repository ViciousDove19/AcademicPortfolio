# PS-MAE

<Stamp>Under review, IEEE JBHI</Stamp>

*A clinically-informed peritumoral masked autoencoder for axillary lymph
node status assessment from breast ultrasound.*

## The gist

Here is the clinical fact this whole project starts from. Whether breast
cancer has spread to the axillary lymph nodes is not written only in the
tumour itself. Radiologists also read the tissue right around it. Margin
irregularity matters. Subtle changes in the surrounding tissue matter too.

Most imaging models never get the chance to learn any of that. They get
pretrained by masking random patches anywhere in the image and
reconstructing raw pixels. That process spends most of its capacity on
ultrasound speckle. It spends almost none of it on anything actually
diagnostic.

PS-MAE changes two things at once. It masks a ring of tissue drawn only
around the tumour. Not the lesion itself. Not random patches. And instead
of asking the model to redraw pixels, it asks the model to reconstruct the
edges inside that ring. Edges survive speckle noise. Raw pixels do not. On
their own, neither change moves the needle much. Put together, they do.

## Research question

Confirming whether cancer has already reached the axillary lymph nodes
currently means a biopsy. That is invasive, so predicting the answer from
an ultrasound scan instead would spare a lot of patients an unnecessary
procedure. Two things get in the way of doing that well.

Labelled data for this exact question is scarce, because collecting it
means running the invasive test in the first place. Ultrasound is also a
noisy signal on its own. Its low signal-to-noise ratio means most
self-supervised pretraining ends up learning the noise, not the
peritumoral tissue that actually carries the prognostic signal. So the
real question here is not just whether ultrasound can predict nodal
status. It is whether a pretraining scheme can be pointed at the right
tissue from the start, instead of learning whatever pattern happens to be
easiest to reconstruct.

## Solution

PS-MAE starts by teaching a U-Net to find the tumour-like region on a
large pool of unlabeled breast-ultrasound images. Once that boundary
exists, it gets dilated outward by a few patches. That dilated band is the
peritumoral ring, and it is the only part of the image PS-MAE masks during
pretraining. Random patches never get touched.

Instead of reconstructing raw pixel values inside that ring, the model
reconstructs the Sobel edge-gradient response of the masked region. That
shift matters, because edges emphasise boundary and texture structure,
which is closer to what a radiologist actually looks at. Raw pixel values
mostly just chase speckle.

<Figure
  src="/research/ps-mae/pixel-vs-sobel.webp"
  alt="Two samples comparing the peritumoral ring against a raw-pixel reconstruction target and a Sobel edge-gradient reconstruction target."
  title="What the model is asked to reconstruct: pixels versus edges"
  caption="The pixel target (b) keeps every grey-level value inside the ring, speckle included. The Sobel target (c) keeps only edge structure, which is what actually distinguishes tumour margin from surrounding tissue."
/>

The encoder does not start from ImageNet weights either. It starts from
USFM, a model already trained on ultrasound. That choice means training
time goes toward learning the peritumoral signal, not toward first
unlearning natural-image statistics.

Once pretraining finishes, the decoder is discarded. The frozen encoder is
evaluated with a linear probe on labelled nodal-status data. Attention
rollout is then used as a check afterward. It shows how much of the
model's attention actually lands inside the peritumoral ring it was
trained to look at.

## Results

Pretraining pooled 83,271 unlabeled frames from 11 public breast-ultrasound
sources. Downstream evaluation used an internal cohort of 698 images from
175 patients, plus a fully held-out external cohort from AIIMS New Delhi,
88 patients and 176 images, that never appeared anywhere in pretraining.

On the internal cohort, PS-MAE was tested against seven baselines. Two
were generic self-supervised models, DINOv2 and an ImageNet-pretrained
network. Two more were already trained specifically on ultrasound, USFM
and USF-MAE. PS-MAE came out ahead of all of them.

Two ablations explain where the gain actually comes from. First, the
masking region and the reconstruction target only help when they work
together. Sobel edges alone barely move the needle. The peritumoral ring
alone barely moves it either. Combine the two, and the result clearly
beats either piece on its own.

## What next

The clearest benefit here is that PS-MAE holds up somewhere it was never
trained. Most models lose most of their edge the moment they leave the
hospital that produced their training data. PS-MAE keeps a meaningful
share of its advantage on a hospital it never saw during pretraining, and
the attention-rollout result explains why. It is genuinely looking at the
tissue radiologists look at, not just tissue that happens to resemble its
training distribution.

That property matters most anywhere a diagnostic model has to work across
hospitals with different scanners and different patient populations,
which describes most real deployment settings. A model that quietly
degrades the moment it changes hospitals is a much bigger practical risk
than one whose accuracy ceiling is a point or two lower.

Only one external institution has validated this so far, so the next real
test is broader multi-centre validation across more than one unseen
hospital. The ring itself is also still a fixed width right now. Letting
that width adapt to tumour size and shape, instead of using one dilation
amount for every case, is the next step before this moves closer to
something usable in the clinic.
