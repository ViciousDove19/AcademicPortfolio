## In plain language

Ultrasound foundation models keep converging on the same shape: a
Vision Transformer with roughly 86 million parameters. That's fine in a
research lab, but it will never run on the hardware point-of-care
ultrasound actually happens on — a laptop, a phone, a handheld probe's own
compute. A prior paper (TinyUSFM) already showed you *can* compress these
models down, but it took an elaborate pipeline to do it: curating a
200,000-image subset, a domain-separated masking scheme, dynamically
weighted consistency losses. This project asks a more basic question first:
how much of that machinery is actually necessary?

The answer it tries is almost stubbornly simple. Freeze a large teacher
model. Show a small student model a masked ultrasound image. Have the
student predict what the teacher would have "seen" at the masked positions
— not the pixels, the teacher's own internal representation of them — plus
one small auxiliary head on top. No curated subset, no dynamic weighting,
trained on the full uncurated corpus.

## Overview

Point-of-care ultrasound is exactly the setting where automated
interpretation would matter most, and exactly the setting current
ultrasound foundation models can't run in. This project's question isn't
just "can we compress the model" — TinyUSFM already answered that — but
which parts of a heavier compression pipeline were genuinely necessary, and
which downstream tasks quietly stop working once you shrink the model.

## Method

A frozen 86M-parameter ViT-B teacher (USF-MAE) supervises a 5.7M-parameter
ViT-Tiny student. Input patches are masked at a 0.3–0.5 ratio; the student
encodes the masked image, and a cross-attention predictor regresses the
teacher's embeddings at exactly the masked positions, under a Smooth-L1
loss. One auxiliary head on the pooled student feature adds a small
supervised signal on top. That's the entire recipe — deliberately two
components instead of the prior pipeline's several, trained on an
uncurated corpus rather than a hand-picked subset.

## Data

532,752 B-mode ultrasound images spanning 18 organs and 54 acquisition
sources, all public datasets, deduplicated by perceptual hash. Evaluated on
seven classification and nine segmentation datasets, checked for overlap
with the pretraining corpus.

So compression is nearly free for whole-image classification, but
genuinely costly for dense, pixel-level prediction. A separate analysis of
the *shape* of what each model learned turned up something the plan itself
calls a genuine surprise: a model's usable representational rank has no
measurable correlation with its downstream accuracy, and the relationship
between rank and robustness to image corruption actually reverses sign
depending on whether you're comparing across different models or shrinking
one model's own representation — a reminder that some intuitive-sounding
representation-quality metrics don't transfer the way you'd expect.

**Not yet measured:** the actual efficiency numbers — GFLOPs, latency,
memory — that would make the deployment argument concrete. The plan flags
this explicitly as the single highest-priority missing experiment, so it's
stated here as pending rather than filled in with a guess.
