## The gist

Ultrasound foundation models keep converging on the same shape. A Vision
Transformer with roughly 86 million parameters. That is fine inside a
research lab. It will never run on the hardware point-of-care ultrasound
actually happens on, a laptop, a phone, or a handheld probe's own onboard
compute. A prior paper, TinyUSFM, already showed these models can be
compressed. But it took an elaborate pipeline to get there: a curated
200,000-image subset, a domain-separated masking scheme, and dynamically
weighted consistency losses. This project asks a more basic question
first. How much of that machinery is actually necessary?

The answer it tries is almost stubbornly simple. Freeze a large teacher
model. Show a small student model a masked ultrasound image. Have the
student predict what the teacher would have seen at the masked positions.
Not the pixels themselves, but the teacher's own internal representation
of them. Add one small auxiliary head on top of that, and stop there. No
curated subset. No dynamic weighting. Train on the full, uncurated corpus.

## Research question

Point-of-care ultrasound is exactly the setting where automated
interpretation would matter most. It is also exactly the setting current
ultrasound foundation models cannot run in. TinyUSFM already proved that
compression itself is possible. That is not the question this project is
asking.

The real question is which parts of a heavier compression pipeline were
genuinely necessary, and which downstream tasks quietly stop working the
moment the model gets smaller. A recipe that is simpler to build and train
is only worth adopting if it does not silently break something a
clinician would actually rely on.

## Solution

A frozen 86-million-parameter ViT-B teacher, USF-MAE, supervises a
5.7-million-parameter ViT-Tiny student. Input patches are masked at a
ratio between 0.3 and 0.5. The student encodes the masked image, and a
cross-attention predictor regresses the teacher's embeddings at exactly
the masked positions, under a Smooth-L1 loss. One auxiliary head sits on
top of the pooled student feature, adding a small supervised signal.

That is the entire recipe. Two components, not the prior pipeline's
several. Trained on an uncurated corpus rather than a hand-picked subset.
The bet behind this design is that most of what made the earlier,
heavier pipeline work was never the curation or the dynamic weighting in
the first place. It was the basic act of making a small model predict a
large model's internal representation.

## Results

Training used 532,752 B-mode ultrasound images spanning 18 organs and 54
acquisition sources, all from public datasets, deduplicated by perceptual
hash. Evaluation covered seven classification datasets and nine
segmentation datasets, each checked for overlap with the pretraining
corpus first.

Classification is where the recipe's simplicity pays off most clearly.
The numbers below are reported relative to baselines, since that is how
the draft plan states them.

At the pooled-feature level, the compact student is not just close to the
much larger models it learned from. Fine-tuned, it is statistically
indistinguishable from four separate 86-million-parameter baselines.

That parity does not extend to segmentation, where the compact model
genuinely trails the larger encoders it was distilled from.

So compression is nearly free for whole-image classification, but it
genuinely costs something for dense, pixel-level prediction. A separate
analysis looked at the shape of what each model actually learned, and it
turned up a real surprise, one the plan itself calls out directly. A
model's usable representational rank has no measurable correlation with
its downstream accuracy. Stranger still, the relationship between rank
and robustness to image corruption flips sign depending on whether you
are comparing different models to each other or shrinking one model's own
representation. Some representation-quality metrics that sound intuitive
simply do not transfer the way you would expect them to.

**Not yet measured.** The actual efficiency numbers, GFLOPs, latency, and
memory, that would make the deployment argument concrete are still
missing. The plan flags this as the single highest-priority remaining
experiment, so it is stated here as pending rather than filled in with a
guess.

## What next

If the efficiency numbers land where the parameter count suggests they
should, the practical case here is straightforward. A model that matches
much larger encoders on classification, at a fraction of the size, is
exactly what point-of-care hardware needs for the tasks it is most often
asked to do, like triage and quick screening reads. That is the direct
path to real deployment on a phone or a handheld probe.

Segmentation is the open problem. The gap there suggests dense,
pixel-level prediction needs either a larger student or a distillation
signal that targets spatial detail directly, rather than a single pooled
representation. Closing that gap, or clearly scoping the compact model to
tasks where it already matches its teacher, is the natural next step.

Before any of that, though, the missing efficiency measurement has to get
taken. A compression story without latency and memory numbers is
incomplete by definition, and the plan already treats it as the top
priority.
